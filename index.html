The Beginning

Building a drone is a complex process. From sourcing components and integrating subsystems to achieving that first successful flight, every step presents a new challenge.

We started by building a DIY Drone Kit and eventually went on to develop our own flight controller and remote controller. Over 18 months, we built three major flight controller iterations and invested Rs. 4 lakhs in indigenous development.

We were aerospace engineers building the core of the drone ecosystem. Without realizing it, we were solving a problem that was not urgent enough. Or perhaps we were simply deaf to what everyone around us had already been telling us. Identity trap

The Pivot We Did Not Recognize

We conducted Build Your Own Drone workshops, and the response was strong. Parents referred other parents. Teachers reached out. Revenue came in consistently. Yet we could not clearly see what was working or how to scale it.

DJI was our north star, and we saw ourselves as a product company. So we built a DIY drone kit, priced it at Rs. 5,000, and watched it get compared endlessly to imported Chinese drones. It felt like a race to the bottom. A race we had chosen to enter ourselves. Quiet signal neglect

The Question That Cut Through Everything

My father asked a simple question: "What is actually bringing in revenue?"

That one question cut through 18 months of narrative. The workshops had been the answer from day one. The kit was the instrument. The experience was the product. We were a B2B2C hardware plus edtech company, not a D2C hardware company. We had been validating the wrong thing all along. Real signal found

What I Built From It

This toolkit exists because first-time founders in hardware, edtech, and hybrid hardware plus edtech businesses often lack a systematic way to interpret the signals their market is already giving them.

It is designed to challenge founder bias, surface hidden patterns, and help founders see the reality that is already right in front of them.

Step 4 of 5
6 questions. Be honest.
No one is watching. These reveal how you are unconsciously filtering what you see.
1. Are your customers coming for you personally, or for the product or service?
2. Did you avoid asking a follow-up question in a customer conversation because you were afraid of what the answer might be?
3. Is your most consistent, quiet signal, the one that keeps showing up without you chasing it, getting less attention than a louder but one-off signal?
4. If your product disappeared tomorrow, would this customer change their workflow, or just go back to what they did before?
5. Why are your first 10 customers buying, and would the next 100 buy for the same reason?
6. If a competitor copies your product exactly and sells it for 30% less, what happens to your customers?
